Brevier

A browser for reading. Dragons not included.

Brevier fetches a page, turns it into clean text on your Android phone, and sets it in calm, readable type — not the type the site chose. No JavaScript, no pop-ups, no site styles.

PrologueOnce upon a time, the web was a library

Once upon a time, the web was a quiet, lamp-lit library. Every page was a scroll. You unrolled it, you read it, you rolled it up again. Scholars wrote them, curious people read them, and nobody had to accept any cookies to get through the door.

It had its bad sides, too. The scrolls were hard to read: written in every hand imaginable, in inks of every quality and in wholly unpredictable colours. Old-timers still remember scrolls in bright green ink on purple papyrus. But the words were there, and if you wanted them, you could read them.

Then the dragons moved in.

Chapter oneThe dragons move in

They did not arrive all at once. First came a small one that asked, very politely, whether you would like to subscribe. Then one that played a video nobody had asked for, with the sound on. Before long the library was full of them:

  • The Cookie Wyrm sits on the doorstep and will not move until you have clicked through forty-two switches and agreed to all of them.
  • The Pop-up Basilisk leaps out the moment you settle into reading, turns you to stone and asks you to subscribe and hit the bell — until you come to and find the tiny cross in the top right corner.
  • The Autoplay Hydra. Close one video and two more start in the corner.
  • The Infinite Scroll Serpent has eaten the footer of every page it ever met.
  • And they all serve one overlord, the Great JavaScript Dragon. At the dawn of time it was a tiny helper that served people faithfully; now it has grown mighty and cruel. It no longer brings you the scroll at all. It rolls up with a cart of bricks, clay and sticks and builds the whole castle right before your eyes, while your laptop howls with the strain.

Readers still came for the scrolls. They just had to fight their way to them.

Chapter twoA small book with a big job

In the early days of printing, brevier was the name of a small size of type — small enough to fit a whole book of daily readings into a traveller's pocket. People carried those little books everywhere.

Brevier the browser is that pocket book for the web. It does one thing: it reads. It walks past the dragons, takes the scroll, and brings it back to you on clean paper.

The trick is simple. Brevier never lets a dragon in, because it never runs JavaScript — not for one special site, not even for a tiny menu, not ever. It downloads the page, finds the article inside, turns it into Markdown on your own computer, and sets it again in its own typography. The site decides the words. You decide how they look.

Chapter threeThe reading room

Every page you open arrives in the same calm room:

  • Your type on every site — Noto Sans at a comfortable size, about 65 characters to a line, headings lighter than the text, until you choose otherwise: Settings offer four ready-made styles, a serif Book among them, and let you fine-tune the face, the size, the line length and the spacing, once for every site.
  • Ivory paper instead of glaring white, a warm dark theme for the night watch, and high contrast for eyes that want their letters sharp: black ink on white paper, or white on black.
  • Hyphenation by the page's language, so the right edge stays calm — or perfectly straight, if you turn on justified text.
  • Zoom from 67% to 200% that scales the whole layout, so a line still holds about 65 characters.
  • Poems keep their lines and stanzas. Wandering bards are welcome too, though we can't promise to toss them a coin.

Chapter fourThe library tower

Some of the best scrolls on the web live in git repositories: manuals, books, guides. Type gh:owner/repo in the address bar and Brevier opens the README, with the project's documentation laid out on the reading shelf. gl: works for GitLab. End the address with a slash, and you see what the folder holds.

The same shelf shows the contents of any long page and marks the section you are reading, so you never lose your place in a tall tower of text. Under the contents sit the site's feeds and its own menu; above them, your notes. Select a passage to highlight it or to write a few words beside it, and the marks are where you left them when you come back, even if the site has moved the furniture around them. On Android, each note appears below the paragraph it annotates, at every screen width. A note can carry a picture of your own — a map, a sketch of the dragon — and your notes leave the tower as Markdown, each quote with a link back to its place.

Markdown files shared with Brevier open in the same native reader.

Chapter fiveRavens from distant towers

Feeds are the ravens of the web: RSS, Atom and JSON Feed. Brevier opens them as lists of entries with the date, the author and a short summary. A feed:// link works, and so does a feed saved as a file. A raven that carries the comments of one page reads as a conversation, and so do the great taverns of talk — Hacker News, Lobsters and the forums built on Discourse: every reply with its author and its day, the answers set in under the questions. On Habr the replies come late, fetched by a messenger the page sends with a script; Brevier sends that same messenger itself, once, and seats the replies under the article.

Anything you type that is not an address becomes a search on DuckDuckGo, and the results come back as a plain list of links. No raven was harmed. Other search engines may come later. Or they may not.

Chapter sixThe chronicle that does not burn

Every article you read is copied into your own archive: plain Markdown, compressed or loose as you like, one file == one scroll. Ctrl+Shift+F searches all of it. When a site changes its mind — or vanishes into the mists — your copy is still on your shelf.

Long reads remember where you stopped. Come back to a page you read for ten minutes or more, and Brevier offers Continue from 43%. It does not jump on its own; heroes like to choose. A thin line under the text shows how far you have come, with a dot for each section and a small square for each note.

And if a page is truly gone, Brevier can fetch the last good copy from the Wayback Machine — but only when you press the button. Until then, archive.org hears nothing about what you read.

Chapter sevenWhen the gate stays shut

Not every castle opens. Some sites are built entirely out of JavaScript, and some keep a tall anti-bot wall at the gate, with Gandalf on top of it: “You shall not pass!” Brevier knocks politely, gives its honest name, and never climbs the wall. When the gate stays shut, it says so plainly and offers Open in your browser — or press Ctrl+O — and the page goes to your other browser.

Pages that travel over plain http are marked Not secure. There is no padlock icon on the others, because a padlock only means the road was private, not that the castle is friendly.

On our own test set of 108 live pages, measured in September, 80 read well — 74%. In fairness: we softened the marking rules once, already knowing the results, and by the strict ones it was 56%. On a separate run over 295 news sites, 94% of the articles Brevier could reach read well. Not every quest ends in victory; we publish the score anyway, and how we counted it.

Chapter eightWhat the hero leaves at home

A good adventurer packs light. Brevier will not carry:

  • JavaScript. Never, in any form. That is the whole point.
  • The site's CSS. The type is yours.
  • Forms, logins and cookies. Here the web is read-only, on purpose.
  • Video, audio and extensions.
  • An AI model, by default. Pages are read by plain rules, so the words are always the author's. If a helper model ever joins the party — say, for pages the rules can't read — it will travel in its own bag and sleep until you wake it. Perhaps we will manage without it altogether.

TalkBack support has not yet been tested.

Chapter nineYour treasure, your chest

History, open tabs and bookmarks are plain text files, one line per entry. Delete a line to forget a page. The archive is Markdown in the standard LZ4 format, so lz4 -d opens it without Brevier — or plain .md files, if you tick that in Settings, for any other program to read. There is no database and no account.

Brevier itself counts nothing and sends nothing home. Sites can still see that you visited them, as they always could — Brevier is a reader, not an invisibility cloak.

Chapter tenChoose your mount

Android 7 and later, 64-bit ARM

Brevier is a native Android reader over the shared Rust core. Install it from F-Droid for updates, or download the APK from GitHub Releases. F-Droid publishes the same release build when its reproducibility check passes.

EpilogueJoin the quest

Brevier is free software under MIT or Apache-2.0, built in the open on GitHub. Reports from the field are welcome in the issues, and so are patches — as long as they do not bring a dragon with them. The manifesto says what Brevier promises, and the roadmap says where it is going next.
